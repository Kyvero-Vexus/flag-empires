* Style Bible


** Purpose
This codex governs diction, cadence, chronology, and referential method for the Flag-Empires corpus. The tone is high chronicle, grave but lucid, with periodic lyrical expansion. Every chapter shall move between three registers: annal, witness, and lament.

** Core Register
- Prefer long, rolling sentences for historical sweep.
- Break into short, hard clauses for battle, famine, betrayal, and decree.
- Keep names consistent by canonical form.
- Use archaic flavor sparingly; clarity is law.

* Arc 1: 'The Wind Before Names'
The world was cloth before it was cloth. In the earliest memory-cycles, the Dawn Age, banners were wind's signatures on stone. Tribes who traced the migrations of birds found that cloth, when left to weather on high peaks, absorbed the memory of storms. They called it "storm-laughter" and wore it as a mark of having survived the year's first gale. The clans of the Dry Hills recorded their ages not by suns but by the number of cloths they had tattered and rewoven. [Canon: FE-DA-B1-ANN-001 | Year: DA 1]

The Hill clans were never one people. Each valley named itself after the sound its river made against rock. The people of Silver-Shriek built their first permanent markers from bundled reeds painted with ochre spirals. The folk of Deep-Grumble raised stone cairns that whistled in crosswinds. When clans met at the Salt-Gift Fairs, they exchanged cloths dyed with the same mineral but folded in different counts. The Salt-Gift was both trade and oath: salt for cloth, cloth for salt, salt to seal the oath, oath to bind the salt. [Canon: FE-DA-B1-ANN-002 | Year: DA 12]

No banner yet flew above a mast. Instead, cloths were tied to the horns of sacred oxen that walked the borders between clans. To follow the ox was to accept the cloth's judgment. When two cloths met at a crossroads, the ox would kneel toward the cloth whose dye held the deepest mineral stain. That clan claimed the crossroads for one turning of the moon. The ox-judges never spoke; they only knelt or stood. Their silence was the first law. [Canon: FE-DA-B1-ANN-003 | Year: DA 23]

The Salt Courts emerged when the river mouths silted and the inland clans could no longer reach the sea. A council of salt-merchants from five valleys built a raft of bound cedar and floated it into the tide. There they laid out salt cakes in a circle and waited for the gulls to choose where to land. The landing pattern dictated the price of salt for the next season. Merchants who cheated the pattern found their salt shriveling in the cask before the moon waned. The Salt Courts judged not only salt but also the legitimacy of cloth exchanges. A cloth without mineral dye was called "wind-lie" and could not be traded. [Canon: FE-DA-B1-ANN-004 | Year: DA 31]

The first great memory-cycle collapse came when a drought dried the riverbeds for three turning-seasons. Clans hoarded salt and cloth alike. The ox-judges wandered aimlessly, their horns stripped of dyed tassels. In the year of black springs, a child named Ash-Cloth wandered from the Silver-Shriek valley carrying a strip of undyed linen. She walked to the salt-raft and laid the cloth upon the salt circle. No gull landed. The cloth remained untouched until the next moon, when a single drop of rain fell upon it. The drop left a dark circle, and the merchants declared the cloth blessed. Ash-Cloth became the first Seal-Keeper, keeper of cloths that had survived calamity. [Canon: FE-DA-B1-ANN-005 | Year: DA 47]

The Seal-Keeper's role was to record which cloths had been marked by storm, drought, or flood. She kept a ledger of mineral stains and their meanings. A cloth with three concentric circles meant the clan had survived three great storms. A cloth with a single dark streak meant the river had risen to the roof-beams. These records were kept in clay jars sealed with wax and buried beneath the oldest cairn. When a Seal-Keeper died, her successor had to dig up the jars and recite the stains aloud before the first snow. Failure meant the clan would lose its right to trade salt for the next year. [Canon: FE-DA-B1-ANN-006 | Year: DA 53]

The memory-cycles were not always peaceful. In the year of the raven-cloth war, two clans claimed the same mineral spring. Each dyed their cloths with the spring's water and declared the color sacred. The ox-judges refused to kneel, for both cloths bore the same hue. The clans resorted to counting the number of times each cloth flapped in a crosswind. The cloth that flapped more often was declared victorious. The losing clan had to surrender its salt stores and weave a new cloth in the winner's pattern. This counting of flaps became known as the "wind-count verdict." [Canon: FE-DA-B1-ANN-007 | Year: DA 61]

The first mast was not a mast at all but a lightning-struck tree left standing in the valley of Stone-Whistle. The tree's top was sheared off, leaving a smooth pole. A traveler from the east hung a strip of red cloth from it, claiming the color honored a bird whose cry warned of storms. Other travelers added their own cloths: blue for river memory, yellow for sun-heat, white for snow. The tree became a gathering place, and the cloths a record of who had passed. The Seal-Keeper of Stone-Whistle declared the tree a "memory-pole" and forbade its cutting. [Canon: FE-DA-B1-ANN-008 | Year: DA 74]

The memory-pole's influence spread. Clans began to erect their own poles, each with a single cloth at first. The cloths were not yet banners; they were "pole-tokens." A pole-token could be challenged only by another pole-token of equal size and dye. Challenges were settled by having both cloths flap in a gale; the one that tore first lost the pole. This practice led to the first pole-wars, where entire valleys fought to keep their pole-tokens aloft. The wars ended when the Seal-Keeper of Stone-Whistle decreed that pole-tokens could only be challenged once per turning-season. [Canon: FE-DA-B1-ANN-009 | Year: DA 82]

The year of the three floods brought a new understanding of cloth. When the rivers rose, they carried debris that snagged on the memory-poles. The debris—reeds, branches, animal hides—became tangled with the pole-tokens. Survivors noticed that the most resilient pole-tokens were those woven with multiple fibers. Clans began to weave cloths from wool, hemp, and river-reeds together. These "mixed-cloths" were said to hold the strength of all three elements. The Seal-Keeper of Stone-Whistle declared that mixed-cloths could not be challenged, for they represented unity. [Canon: FE-DA-B1-ANN-010 | Year: DA 97]

The first true banner rose in the valley of Wind-Cut when a storm tore a pole-token into three pieces. The clan's weaver sewed the pieces back together with a thread of gold wire given as a gift from a passing merchant. The repaired cloth was larger than before and caught the wind with a deeper sound. The clan declared it a "storm-banner" and flew it from a newly raised mast. Other clans mocked the storm-banner as "patched pride" until a drought struck Wind-Cut and the storm-banner was seen to sweat droplets of moisture. The droplets were collected and used to bless the clan's crops. [Canon: FE-DA-B1-ANN-011 | Year: DA 104]

The storm-banner's success led to a new era of mast-building. Clans competed to erect the tallest masts, believing height brought favor from the wind. The mast of Stone-Whistle was raised to twice the height of a man. The mast of Deep-Grumble reached three times a man's height. The mast of Silver-Shriek was felled by a lightning strike before it could be completed. The Seal-Keeper of Wind-Cut decreed that no mast could exceed the height of the tallest tree in the valley. This decree was ignored, and the mast-wars began. [Canon: FE-DA-B1-ANN-012 | Year: DA 119]

The mast-wars ended not with a battle but with a fire. A mast in the valley of Ember-River caught fire from a lightning strike and fell across the river, igniting the reeds on both banks. The fire spread to three valleys before the clans united to create a firebreak. In the aftermath, the Seal-Keeper of Stone-Whistle called a council of all mast-keepers. They agreed to limit mast height and to share the responsibility of watching for lightning strikes. The council also agreed that each mast should fly a cloth bearing the colors of all clans, a "unity-cloth." [Canon: FE-DA-B1-ANN-013 | Year: DA 133]

The unity-cloth was a compromise that satisfied no one. Clans still preferred their own colors, and the unity-cloth was often left to rot at the mast's peak while smaller, clan-specific cloths flew below it. The Seal-Keeper of Wind-Cut proposed a new system: each clan would fly its own cloth, but the unity-cloth would be raised only during the Salt-Gift Fairs. This system was accepted, and the unity-cloth became a symbol of temporary truce rather than lasting peace. [Canon: FE-DA-B1-ANN-014 | Year: DA 148]

The first recorded banner-lord was a woman named Cloud-Rider who lived in the valley of Sky-Lean. She was a mast-keeper who also knew the art of weaving cloths that could catch the faintest breeze. Cloud-Rider led her clan in a dispute with the neighboring valley of Rock-Quiet over the right to a salt spring. Instead of challenging the pole-token, Cloud-Rider flew a new cloth from her mast—a cloth so large it could be seen from the next valley. The cloth bore a symbol of a bird in flight, and it seemed to move even when there was no wind. The people of Rock-Quiet surrendered the salt spring without a fight, claiming the bird-symbol was an omen. [Canon: FE-DA-B1-ANN-015 | Year: DA 162]

Cloud-Rider's victory marked the beginning of the banner-lord era. Clans began to seek out weavers who could create cloths with symbols that inspired awe or fear. The Seal-Keeper of Stone-Whistle tried to regulate these symbols, but the weavers formed their own guild and refused to submit their designs for approval. The guild declared that symbols were the domain of the wind and could not be owned by any clan. This declaration led to the first symbol-wars, where clans fought over the right to use certain images on their cloths. [Canon: FE-DA-B1-ANN-016 | Year: DA 177]

The symbol-wars ended when a drought struck the valleys and the weavers could no longer find the plants needed to create certain dyes. The shortage forced clans to share symbols and even colors. The Seal-Keeper of Wind-Cut declared a truce and ordered the weavers to create a new set of symbols that all clans could use. The weavers complied, and the new symbols were simple geometric shapes that could be made with any dye. The truce held for one turning-season, after which the clans returned to their old rivalries. [Canon: FE-DA-B1-ANN-017 | Year: DA 191]

The year of the great wind brought a new understanding of banners. A storm swept through the valleys, tearing down masts and scattering cloths. When the storm passed, the clans found that the cloths which had survived were those that had been allowed to move freely in the wind. The Seal-Keeper of Stone-Whistle declared that banners should not be flown from fixed masts but from poles that could sway with the wind. This new practice, called "wind-dancing," spread quickly. Clans built flexible poles that bent in the wind, and the banners seemed to dance atop them. [Canon: FE-DA-B1-ANN-018 | Year: DA 206]

The wind-dancing banners were a marvel, but they also posed a new problem. The flexible poles were prone to breaking in strong winds, and the banners would be lost. The Seal-Keeper of Wind-Cut proposed a solution: the poles should be anchored not in the ground but in large stones that could roll slightly. This allowed the poles to move without breaking, and the banners could dance more freely. The rolling-stone poles became a new standard, and the clans competed to create the most graceful banner dances. [Canon: FE-DA-B1-ANN-019 | Year: DA 221]

The last memory-cycle of the Dawn Age ended with a great gathering at Stone-Whistle. All the clans came together to celebrate the end of the age and to prepare for the new one. They raised a single mast, taller than any before it, and flew a unity-cloth so large it could be seen from the highest peaks. The Seal-Keeper of Stone-Whistle declared that the Dawn Age had been an age of learning, and that the new age would be one of building. The clans agreed, and they lowered the unity-cloth as a sign of their commitment to the future. [Canon: FE-DA-B1-ANN-020 | Year: DA 233]

* Arc 2: Raising the First Mast-City

The chronicle now turns to the founding of Kher Valaan, the first Mast-City, and the binding of House Talar to river and ridge. In these years, the banner's cloth was still wet with dye, and the wind had not yet learned to speak its name.

* Founding of Kher Valaan

The valley of Kher Valaan was chosen not for its fertility but for its stillness. A river forked here, its two arms meeting in a shallow pool before running south toward the sea. On the eastern bank, a ridge of black stone rose like the back of a sleeping beast. The land had no name, and the people who lived there had no banners. They were hunters and gatherers, moving with the seasons, leaving no mark but fire circles and broken spearheads.

[Canon: FE-FA-B1-ANN-021 | Year: FA 012]
In the season of first thaw, scouts of House Talar came to the fork of the river. They found no city, no king, no temple. Only the black ridge and the water that moved between its feet.

The Talar scouts were led by a woman named Zira, whose eyes were said to be the color of storm clouds. She stood on the ridge and watched the river for three days, noting the patterns of fish, the flight of birds, the way the wind carried the scent of distant forests. On the third night, she dreamed of a mast rising from the stone, a banner snapping in the wind, and a voice that said, *Here you will build.*

When she woke, she sent runners back to the Talar camp with a single word: *Here.*

The Talar arrived not as conquerors but as settlers. They brought cedar logs from the high forests, stone from the ridge, and cloth dyed in indigo and madder. The first structure they raised was not a palace but a watchtower, its base sunk deep into the black stone. From its top, they could see the river in both directions, the forests to the north, and the distant sea to the south.

* House Talar Binds River and Ridge

The binding of river and ridge was a ritual act, performed not by priests but by engineers and weavers. The engineers carved channels in the stone to guide the river's flow, creating basins for fish and mills for grinding grain. The weavers hung banners from the watchtower, each one a different color, each one catching the wind in a different way.

[Canon: FE-FA-B1-ANN-034 | Year: FA 018]
The first banner was blue, the color of the river's depths. It was raised at dawn, and the wind took it like a hand. The second was red, the color of the ridge's stone. It was raised at noon, and the sun made it glow. The third was white, the color of the clouds. It was raised at dusk, and the wind carried it into the dark.

The binding was not only physical but spiritual. The Talar believed that the river and the ridge were living things, and that by building on them, they entered into a covenant. They offered the first fish caught in the new basins, the first grain ground in the new mills, the first cloth woven on the new looms. They sang songs to the river and the ridge, songs that had no words but only tones, rising and falling like water and stone.

* The First Mast Rises

The watchtower became the first mast. It was not tall by later standards, no more than thirty cubits high, but it was the tallest thing for a hundred leagues. On its top, they raised a banner of black and gold, the colors of House Talar. The wind caught it, and it snapped like a whip, and the sound carried across the valley.

[Canon: FE-FA-B1-ANN-045 | Year: FA 023]
On the day the first mast was raised, a storm came from the east. Lightning struck the ridge, and thunder shook the valley. The banner on the mast burned, but the cloth was not consumed. When the storm passed, the banner was still there, black and gold against the gray sky.

The people of the valley watched from the forests and the hills. Some were afraid, thinking the storm a sign of anger. Others were curious, thinking it a sign of power. A few were hopeful, thinking it a sign of change. They came down to the river, carrying their few possessions, their children, their old ones. They asked to join the Talar, to help build the new city.

Zira Talar, who was now the Banner-Lord, welcomed them. She said, *We do not build for ourselves alone. We build for all who come in peace.* She gave them land on the western bank, where they could farm and hunt and fish. She taught them the ways of the river and the ridge, the ways of the banner and the mast.

* The City Grows

Kher Valaan grew not in a single burst but in layers, like the rings of a tree. The first ring was the watchtower and the basins, the mills and the looms. The second ring was the houses of the Talar and their closest allies. The third ring was the houses of the valley people who had joined them. The fourth ring was the market, where goods from the forests and the sea were traded.

[Canon: FE-FA-B1-ANN-056 | Year: FA 030]
In the fifth year of the city, the market was opened. Merchants came from the north with furs and amber, from the east with salt and spices, from the south with shells and pearls. The market was held in a square at the foot of the ridge, and the banner of House Talar flew over it day and night.

The city needed laws, and Zira Talar gave them. She said, *The river belongs to all, but the channels belong to those who dig them. The ridge belongs to all, but the houses belong to those who build them. The market belongs to all, but the scales belong to those who weigh.* She appointed judges to settle disputes, and they sat under the banner of House Talar, their decisions binding as the wind.

* The First Harvest

The first harvest was a time of joy and fear. The valley people had planted the fields with seeds given by the Talar, seeds of wheat and barley, beans and squash. They did not know if the crops would grow, or if they would be enough. When the fields turned gold and green, when the beans hung like pearls, when the squash lay on the ground like stones, they knew they had been blessed.

[Canon: FE-FA-B1-ANN-067 | Year: FA 037]
On the day of the first harvest, a feast was held in the market square. The Talar provided wine and meat, the valley people provided bread and vegetables. They ate together, sang together, danced together. The banner of House Talar flew over the feast, and the wind made it ripple like water.

But the feast was also a warning. Zira Talar stood on the steps of the watchtower and said, *This harvest is not the end. It is the beginning. Next year, the river may flood. The ridge may shake. The wind may bring fire. We must be ready.* She ordered the building of granaries, the digging of wells, the weaving of nets. She said, *We store not for ourselves but for all who may come to us in need.*

* The First Winter

The first winter was hard, harder than any had expected. The river froze, the ridge was buried in snow, the wind howled like a beast. The granaries were full, but the people were hungry. The nets were woven, but the fish were gone. The houses were warm, but the cold came through the walls.

[Canon: FE-FA-B1-ANN-078 | Year: FA 042]
In the heart of winter, a child died of fever. His mother was from the valley, his father was a Talar. They buried him under the ridge, where the snow was shallow. They marked the grave with a stone, and on the stone they hung a small banner, white with a black border. It was the first funeral banner in Kher Valaan.

Zira Talar came to the grave. She said, *We mourn, but we do not despair. The river will thaw. The ridge will stand. The wind will change.* She ordered the building of a temple at the foot of the ridge, a place where the living could speak to the dead, where the river could be honored, where the banner could be kept safe.

* The Temple of the Banner

The temple was not like the watchtower or the houses. It was round, with a roof of cedar shakes and walls of woven reed. In the center, a fire was kept burning day and night, fed by wood from the forests. Around the fire, banners were hung, each one representing a season, a river, a ridge, a house, a person.

[Canon: FE-FA-B1-ANN-089 | Year: FA 048]

Here we honor the dead and the living. Here we keep the banner safe.* She hung the black and gold banner of House Talar on the eastern wall, where the rising sun would touch it first.

The temple became the center of the city's life. People came to pray, to seek advice, to make offerings. They brought the first fruits of their fields, the first fish of the season, the first cloth from their looms. They asked for blessings on their houses, their children, their work. They asked for protection from the river's floods, the ridge's quakes, the wind's fires.

* The Second Mast

As Kher Valaan grew, so did its need for more masts. The first mast was the watchtower, but it was not enough. The city needed masts on the western bank, on the southern fields, on the northern forests. Each mast was a tower, each tower a watchpoint, each watchpoint a link in the chain of the banner's reach.

[Canon: FE-FA-B1-ANN-090 | Year: FA 053]
The second mast was raised on the western bank, opposite the first. It was built by the valley people, under the guidance of Talar engineers. It was not as tall as the first, no more than twenty cubits, but it was strong. On its top, they raised a banner of green and white, the colors of the valley. The wind took it, and it snapped like a whip, and the sound carried across the river.

The raising of the second mast was a day of celebration. The Banner-Lord Zira Talar came to the western bank, and she spoke to the valley people. She said, *This mast is yours. This banner is yours. This city is yours, as much as it is ours.* She ordered the building of a bridge between the two banks, a bridge of stone and wood, strong enough to hold the weight of carts and horses.

The bridge was finished before the next winter. It was the first bridge in the valley, the first link between the two sides of the river. It was a symbol of the city's unity, of the covenant between the Talar and the valley people, of the binding of river and ridge.

* The City's Voice

Kher Valaan now had a voice, and it was the voice of the banner. The banner spoke in the wind, in the snap of cloth, in the color of dye. It spoke of the river's flow, the ridge's strength, the city's growth. It spoke of the past and the future, of the dead and the living, of the covenant and the promise.

[Canon: FE-FA-B1-ANN-101 | Year: FA 060]
In the seventh year after the bridge, a stranger came to Kher Valaan. He was from the south, from a city of stone and glass, where the banners were silk and the masts were iron. He brought news of the world beyond the valley, of kings and empires, of wars and trade. He spoke of a great river to the south, wider than any in the valley, and of a sea beyond it, where the sun set in fire.

The stranger was welcomed by the Banner-Lord Zira Talar. She listened to his stories, asked him questions, gave him food and shelter. She said, *We are small, but we are strong. We are few, but we are many. We are here, and we will stay.* She gave him a banner of black and gold, a small one, to take back to his city. She said, *Tell them of Kher Valaan. Tell them of the river and the ridge. Tell them of the covenant and the promise.*

The stranger left, carrying the banner. He went south, toward the great river, toward the sea of fire. He told his stories, and the stories spread. They reached the ears of kings and merchants, of warriors and priests. They spoke of a city in a valley, of a banner in the wind, of a covenant in the stone.

And so Kher Valaan became known, not as a place on a map, but as a name in the wind, a voice in the banner, a light in the dark.

** The Charter of the Crescent Authority

#+begin_quote

The Charter was a bold departure from the inland governance that had prevailed since the First Age. The Admirals, once mere captains of merchant vessels, now wielded the authority of Seal-Keepers over the crescent-shaped coastline that stretched from Saltspire to the eastern cliffs. The Charter established the Crescent Authority as a semi-hereditary body, with the Admirals' Council serving as its legislative arm. The Seal-Keeper, chosen from among the Admirals, would serve as the executive authority, responsible for enforcing the Charter and overseeing the salt courts.

The Charter was not without its critics. Some inland merchants feared that the Admirals' control over coastal trade would lead to higher prices and reduced access to goods. Others worried that the semi-hereditary nature of the Authority would lead to corruption and nepotism. But the Admirals argued that their control over the coast was necessary to ensure the safety of shipping lanes and the stability of trade. #+begin_quote

The salt courts were a novel institution, combining elements of maritime law and traditional jurisprudence. Each court was presided over by a Magistrate, appointed by the Seal-Keeper and confirmed by the Admirals' Council. The courts were funded by a tax on salt, a commodity that was essential to the preservation of food and the maintenance of trade routes. The tax was levied on all salt imported or exported through the crescent coast, and the revenue was used to pay the salaries of the Magistrates and the court staff.

The salt courts quickly became a cornerstone of the Crescent Authority's power. They provided a forum for resolving disputes that might otherwise have led to violence or the breakdown of trade. The courts also served as a check on the power of the Admirals, ensuring that their decisions were subject to judicial review. But the courts were not without their critics. #+TITLE: Arc 3: The Hundred Harbors Crisis
#+AUTHOR: Chronicler of the Crescent Archives

* Prologue: Salt and Steel

The salt courts of the Second Age had grown fat on levies from every merchant hull that entered the Crescent Ports. Yet prosperity bred its own predators. By the year of the Black Tide, the ledger-keepers found their inkwells running dry even as the coin chests overflowed. Debtors multiplied like barnacles on a ship's hull, and the courts, bound by ancient statute, could no longer grant writs of seizure without unanimous consent from three harbor masters. That unanimity, once a pillar of law, became a weapon in the hands of pirate leagues who held entire districts hostage.

The crisis began not with a cannonade but with a ledger. The Fifth Ledger, kept in the sealed vaults beneath the Iron Bell Tower, recorded every harbor's quota of salt tax and trade tariffs. When the ledger's keeper, a woman named Sariel of the Seventh Ledger, vanished in the night, the courts found their authority unmoored. Without the ledger, no harbor could prove its dues had been paid; without proof, no ship could leave port without risking seizure. The Crescent Ports, once the beating heart of Flagistan commerce, ground to a halt.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-ANN-001 | Reliability: R3 | Locale: FE-LOC-B2-004]
In the month of the Black Tide the Fifth Ledger vanished, and with it the certainty of dues. Harbors disputed quotas, and ships rotted at anchor while the salt courts debated in circles.
#+end_quote

* Chapter 1: The Pirate Leagues

The pirate leagues, long dismissed as scourges of the outer seas, saw opportunity in the chaos. They were not a single entity but a constellation of factions: the Red Sails of the northern shoals, the Cutlass Brotherhood of the western reefs, and the Silent Knives who haunted the river deltas. Each league had its own code, but all shared a disdain for the salt courts' levies. When the Fifth Ledger disappeared, they moved to fill the vacuum.

The Red Sails, led by a one-eyed woman known only as the Crimson Widow, blockaded the northern approaches to the Crescent Ports. Their ships, painted in fading scarlet, flew no colors but the black flag of ransom. They offered safe passage to any vessel that paid in advance, and within weeks they controlled the flow of spices, silks, and silver from the northern trade routes.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-WIT-017 | Reliability: R4 | Locale: FE-LOC-B2-009]
I saw the Red Sails' fleet mass off the northern shoals. Their ships moved like a school of sharks, silent and purposeful. The Crimson Widow stood on the deck of her flagship, a woman of iron will, and I heard her offer terms to the harbor master: "Pay now, or starve later."
#+end_quote

The Cutlass Brotherhood, by contrast, operated through terror. They struck at the weakest harbors first, burning docks and sinking grain ships. Their leader, a scarred man called the Hook, left no survivors to tell the tale. Within a month, half the outer harbors had surrendered to his rule, their masters paying tribute in gold and slaves.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-WIT-045 | Reliability: R4 | Locale: FE-LOC-B2-012]
The Hook's men came at dawn. They burned the granaries first, then the docks. I watched from the citadel walls as our harbor master knelt in the ashes, offering his daughter as hostage for our compliance. The Brotherhood's flag rose over our walls that same day.
#+end_quote

* Chapter 2: The Embargo Spirals

As the pirate leagues tightened their grip, the salt courts responded with embargoes. No ship could leave port without a seal of passage from the courts, and no seal could be granted without proof of payment. The courts, desperate to maintain their authority, issued decrees that only deepened the crisis. Merchants, unable to trade, hoarded their goods. Prices soared. Hunger spread through the cities.

The embargo spirals fed on themselves. As trade slowed, the courts' revenues dwindled. As revenues dwindled, the courts could no longer pay their own guards and scribes. Desertions became common. The courts' authority, once absolute, now hung by a thread.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-ANN-023 | Reliability: R3 | Locale: FE-LOC-B2-004]
The embargo deepened. Ships rotted at anchor, their crews unpaid and mutinous. The salt courts, unable to enforce their own decrees, watched as their authority crumbled like sand in the wind.
#+end_quote

The Silent Knives, the most insidious of the pirate leagues, exploited the embargo to their advantage. They infiltrated the courts themselves, bribing scribes and assassinating seal-keepers. By the time the courts realized the extent of the betrayal, the Silent Knives controlled the issuance of seals. They sold passage to the highest bidder, regardless of dues owed.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-WIT-089 | Reliability: R4 | Locale: FE-LOC-B2-004]
I was a scribe in the Iron Bell Tower when the Silent Knives struck. They came at night, their knives flashing in the torchlight. By dawn, the seal-keepers were dead, and the courts' seals were in the hands of the enemy.
#+end_quote

* Chapter 3: The Coup of the Fifth Ledger

The disappearance of the Fifth Ledger had been the spark that ignited the crisis. Its return, or rather its revelation, became the turning point. In the year of the Black Tide's end, a hooded figure appeared before the assembled harbor masters and salt court judges. She claimed to be Sariel of the Seventh Ledger, returned from exile. With her, she brought a new ledger, bound in black leather and sealed with wax.

This ledger, she claimed, was the true Fifth Ledger, recovered from the pirates who had stolen it. But it was not the same ledger. It recorded not the old quotas but new ones, far harsher. Every harbor's dues had been recalculated, and the totals were staggering. The courts, faced with the choice of accepting the new ledger or admitting their own incompetence, chose the former.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-EDI-017 | Reliability: R1 | Locale: FE-LOC-B2-004]
By decree of the Salt Court, the Fifth Ledger is restored. All harbors shall pay the new quotas by the next full moon, under penalty of embargo and seizure.
#+end_quote

The coup was complete. The pirate leagues, who had believed they were acting in their own interests, found themselves bound by the very system they had sought to overthrow. The courts, desperate to reassert their authority, embraced the new ledger as a tool of control. The harbors, faced with impossible demands, had no choice but to comply.

* Chapter 4: The Cost of Order

The restoration of the Fifth Ledger brought a semblance of order, but at a terrible cost. The new quotas were so high that many harbors could not meet them. Those that tried often starved in the attempt. The courts, unable to distinguish between honest failure and deliberate defiance, imposed harsher penalties. Entire districts were razed. Families were sold into slavery.

The pirate leagues, their power broken, either fled or were absorbed into the courts' own enforcement arms. The Red Sails' fleet was burned at anchor. The Cutlass Brotherhood's strongholds were stormed and sacked. The Silent Knives' infiltrators were hunted down and executed. But the victory was hollow. The Crescent Ports, once a beacon of prosperity, now lay in ruins.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-WIT-112 | Reliability: R4 | Locale: FE-LOC-B2-004]
I walked the streets of the Iron Bell Tower after the coup. The markets were empty, the docks silent. The people, once proud and prosperous, now moved like ghosts through the ruins of their own city. The courts had won, but at what cost?
#+end_quote

* Chapter 5: The Last Year

The Hundred Harbors Crisis did not end with the coup of the Fifth Ledger. It merely transformed. The new order, built on fear and extortion, was unsustainable. As the years passed, the courts' authority continued to erode. The harbors, unable to meet the quotas, turned to smuggling and rebellion. The pirate leagues, though broken, left behind a legacy of violence and distrust.

By the time the Last Year arrived, the Crescent Ports were a shadow of their former selves. The Iron Bell Tower stood empty, its seals long since lost. The harbors, once united by trade, were now divided by suspicion and greed. The salt courts, their power shattered, existed only in memory.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-WIT-120 | Reliability: R5 | Locale: FE-LOC-B2-004]
In the Last Year, I saw the final collapse. The Iron Bell Tower fell, its stones scattered by the wind. The harbors, once the pride of Flagistan, were abandoned to the sea. The salt courts, their ledgers burned, were forgotten. Only the pirates remained, their black flags still flying over the ruins.
#+end_quote

* Epilogue: The Weight of Cloth

The Hundred Harbors Crisis left a legacy that outlived the Second Age. The banners that once flew over the Crescent Ports were torn down, their cloth used to bind the wounds of the starving. The salt courts, their seals broken, were replaced by new institutions, but the memory of their tyranny lingered. The pirate leagues, though defeated, became legends, their stories told in taverns and dockside inns.

And the Fifth Ledger? It vanished again, this time for good. Some said it was burned in the riots that followed the Last Year. Others claimed it was taken by the sea, its black leather cover sinking beneath the waves. But in the end, it mattered little. The crisis had already done its work, leaving behind a world forever changed.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-ANN-044 | Year: SA 233]
In the month of westward dust the Fifth Banner crossed the Dry Seam, and no bell was rung in greeting.
#+end_quote

* Losses Uncounted

- The number of lives lost to the embargo spirals and the coup's aftermath can only be estimated.
- The economic cost of the crisis, measured in lost trade and destroyed infrastructure, was incalculable.
- The cultural cost, measured in the erosion of trust and the collapse of institutions, may never be fully understood.

* Arc 3: The Hundred Harbors Crisis

The salt winds had tasted too much blood when the pirate leagues first gathered in the Scar Islands. Their banners flew no allegiance to house or harbors, only to coin and tide. What began as merchant-ship robbery swelled into something more dangerous: organized warfare against the very concept of orderly trade. The Hundred Harbors Crisis would leave half the coastal cities smoldering and end with the Coup of the Fifth Ledger, when the Salt Courts turned their seals against the Banner-Lords themselves.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-WIT-001 | Year: SA 387]
The pirate captain wore a coat of seven stolen colors, each stripe torn from a merchant banner. When we asked her name, she laughed and said she was the daughter of every ship she had ever taken. Her crew cheered, and the gulls overhead wheeled like omens of storm.
#+end_quote

** The Scar Islands Compact

[Canon: FE-SA-B2-WIT-009 | Year: SA 387]

In the autumn of SA 387, nine pirate captains met in the coral fortress of Skull Reef, largest of the Scar Islands. They had come not to divide spoils but to organize rebellion. The merchant fleets of the Hundred Harbors had grown fat on regulated trade routes, their cargo vessels sailing predictable schedules between predictable ports. The pirates called this the "merchant's curse"—too much wealth moving along too few paths.

The Compact they signed was written on sailcloth in squid ink, signed with thumbprints pressed in stolen seal-wax. Each captain would contribute ships to a common fleet, but retain command of their own crews. Raids would be coordinated through a system of signal fires lit on the highest peaks of the Scar Islands. Most significantly, they agreed to target not just individual ships, but entire trade routes.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-WIT-015 | Year: SA 388]
The first coordinated raid struck the Amber Route in three places simultaneously. By dawn, eighteen merchant vessels burned in the harbor of Goldwhisper, and their cargo of northern timber floated like grave-markers on the tide.
#+end_quote

** The Embargo Spirals Begin

[Canon: FE-SA-B2-WIT-023 | Year: SA 389]

The merchant houses responded to pirate attacks as they always had: by raising prices to cover losses. But the Scar Islands Compact had disrupted so many routes that basic goods became scarce in the coastal cities. Salt, the foundation of the Second Age economy, jumped to thrice its normal price when the pirates learned to target the salt barges specifically.

The Banner-Lords tried to break the spiral by hiring more guards for merchant ships, but the additional cost drove prices even higher. Worse, the guards often proved ineffective against the well-coordinated pirate fleets. Some guard captains were revealed to be taking payment from both merchants and pirates, feeding information about ship movements to their supposed enemies.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-WIT-031 | Year: SA 390]
At the fish market in Crescent Harbor, a woman offered to trade her silver bracelet for a handful of salt. The salt-seller refused, saying silver was worth nothing if the salt roads stayed closed. Children began to cry, and their mothers had no salt to preserve food for the winter ahead.
#+end_quote

The embargo spirals fed on themselves. As trade became more dangerous and expensive, fewer merchants were willing to risk their ships. This reduced the supply of goods even further, driving prices higher still. City populations, dependent on trade for everything from food to cloth, began to suffer genuine hardship. The Banner-Lords, whose authority rested on their ability to maintain prosperous commerce, found their legitimacy crumbling with every empty market stall.

** The Salt Courts Turn

[Canon: FE-SA-B2-WIT-042 | Year: SA 392]

The Salt Courts, originally established to regulate maritime trade disputes, found themselves overwhelmed with cases of embargo, piracy, and merchant default. More troubling, they began to uncover evidence that some Banner-Lords were secretly negotiating with pirate captains, offering safe passage for their own ships in exchange for attacks on rivals.

Magistrate Kellan of the Silvermouth Court called for an extraordinary session to investigate these allegations. The evidence was damning: sealed letters promising pirate captains territorial concessions, payments made through intermediaries, and shipping schedules leaked just hours before attacks. The most serious accusation was that Banner-Lord Thane of the Iron Coast had provided the pirates with military-grade weapons in exchange for their promise to blockade his rival's ports.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-WIT-055 | Year: SA 393]
The Banner-Lord of Iron Coast stood before the Salt Court with his hands stained purple—not from noble dye, but from the squid ink of pirate contracts. His own seal-keeper read the charges aloud, and with each word, the accused man seemed to shrink smaller in his ceremonial robes.
#+end_quote

** The Fifth Ledger Conspiracy

[Canon: FE-SA-B2-WIT-067 | Year: SA 394]

The investigation into Banner-Lord corruption led to a more shocking discovery: a secret alliance between corrupt Banner-Lords and the most powerful merchants, coordinated through coded entries in their accounting ledgers. The "Fifth Ledger" was a shadow record that tracked illegal payments, bribery, and collusive price-fixing that had been undermining legitimate trade for years.

The Fifth Ledger revealed that the pirate crisis was not an external threat but a manufactured one. Certain powerful merchants had been funding pirate attacks on their competitors while ensuring their own ships safe passage. The Banner-Lords who participated received shares of the inflated profits that resulted from artificial scarcity.

Most damaging of all, the Fifth Ledger showed that some Salt Court magistrates were also involved, accepting payment to dismiss charges against certain merchants and pirates while prosecuting their victims more aggressively.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-WIT-078 | Year: SA 395]
When they opened the vault beneath the Emerald Harbor courthouse, they found not legal documents but piles of gold and silver, each coin marked with tiny symbols indicating which bribes it represented. The symbols were carved so small that magnifying glasses were needed to read them, and the code was written in ancient script that only the most educated scribes could decipher.
#+end_quote

** The Coup of the Fifth Ledger

[Canon: FE-SA-B2-WIT-089 | Year: SA 396]

The revelation of the Fifth Ledger triggered a revolt within the Salt Courts themselves. Led by Magistrate Kellan and a cohort of uncorrupted judges, the courts declared that they would no longer recognize the authority of any Banner-Lord implicated in the conspiracy. This was unprecedented: the courts had been created by the Banner-Lords, yet now they claimed independent authority to judge their creators.

The "Coup of the Fifth Ledger" was not a military takeover but a legal one. The rebel magistrates seized the official seals of their courts and declared that all contracts signed under the corrupt system were void. They issued warrants for the arrest of seventeen Banner-Lords and demanded the immediate seizure of all assets recorded in the Fifth Ledger.

Several Banner-Lords attempted to resist by calling up their private guards, but the populations of the coastal cities rose in support of the courts. Citizens who had suffered through years of artificial scarcity and inflated prices were eager to see justice done. The guards, many of whom came from ordinary merchant families, refused to fight their own relatives and neighbors.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-WIT-098 | Year: SA 397]
The Banner-Lord of Golden Bay tried to escape by ship, but his own captain—a woman whose father had lost his trading vessel to manufactured piracy—turned the ship around and delivered him to the Salt Courts in chains. She said later that she had signed on to serve a Banner-Lord, not a common thief who wore a crown.
#+end_quote

** The Reckoning

[Canon: FE-SA-B2-WIT-105 | Year: SA 398]

The aftermath of the coup was swift and severe. Thirteen Banner-Lords were stripped of their titles and sentenced to work the salt mines they had once taxed. The pirate captains, deprived of their secret sponsors, found themselves facing a coordinated naval response from the newly honest merchant fleets. Most surrendered within a year, though some fled to distant waters.

The corrupt merchants faced bankruptcy as their manipulated monopolies collapsed and fair competition returned to the markets. Prices of basic goods fell rapidly, but the economic damage of years of artificial scarcity could not be immediately repaired. Many coastal cities required years to rebuild their depleted populations and industries.

The Salt Courts emerged from the crisis with unprecedented power and independence. They were no longer merely trade regulators but had proven themselves capable of judging even the highest authorities. This transformation would have profound consequences for the balance of power in the Second Age.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-WIT-120 | Year: SA 399]
We swept the courthouses clean and built new podiums for the magistrates, raising them higher than the old ones. The Banner-Lords would still bring their disputes to court, but never again would they sit above the judges. The salt and the sea had shown us that justice must flow like water—always seeking the lowest and most honest place.
#+end_quote

The Hundred Harbors Crisis marked the end of the Banner-Lords' unchallenged authority over maritime trade. The Salt Courts had tasted independence and would not willingly surrender it. The Second Age had survived its greatest internal threat, but the power structures that emerged from the crisis would prove unstable in their own way. The seeds of the later administrative partition were sown in the Salt Courts' triumph over corruption, for once the courts could judge Banner-Lords, the question arose: who, if anyone, could judge the courts themselves?

* Arc 4: The Quiet Partition

In the year SA 427, beneath the vaulted silence of the Salt Courts, the Fifth Banner-Lord signed the Partition Charter. No drums sounded. No banners were lowered. The charter was inked in violet—halfway between mourning and triumph—and distributed across four Mast-Cities: Flagartha, Silvermouth, Ironkeep, and Harborhold. Each received a sealed copy, each swore it had always been the rightful capital.

** The Treaty of Four Gates

The document itself was brief, yet its clauses unraveled the empire in silence. The southern salt roads would belong to Flagistan, the northern grain routes to the Iron League, the eastern spice lanes to the Crescent Compact, and the western ports to the Harbor League. In truth, all four polities remained under the nominal banner of the Fifth, but each was permitted its own Seal-Keeper, its own salt tariff, and its own border garrisons.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-ANN-151 | Year: SA 427]
The Fifth Banner-Lord spoke for seven hours, naming each province its own master, yet binding them all to the same mast. No one wept. No one cheered.
#+end_quote

The Partition Charter required that each polity send a child of the Banner-Lord's line to the others as "guest-heir." These hostages were raised in foreign courts, taught rival dialects, and sworn to secrecy about their true parentage. When asked why, the Salt Courts replied: "So no banner may ever be raised against kin."

** The Exchange of Children

In the spring of SA 428, four cradles crossed the empire on four different roads. Each was guarded by the Road-Wardens, yet none knew the contents of the other cradles. The child sent to Flagistan was named Kharad Tines, though he would grow up believing himself the orphaned son of a spice merchant. The child sent to Ironkeep was named Vespera Salt, daughter of no one, heir to nothing.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-ANN-154 | Year: SA 428]
At the third watch the cradles passed each other on the Dry Seam. No lanterns were lit. No songs were sung. The Road-Wardens exchanged no words, only salt tokens.
#+end_quote

In each court, the children were raised with alternating affection and suspicion. They learned the customs of their host cities, adopted local rites, and were forbidden to speak of their origins. The Salt Courts called this the "Quiet Exchange," and it became the model for all future treaties.

** The First Cracks

By SA 432, the cracks in the Partition Charter had begun to show. The salt tariffs in Flagistan had risen to twice the rate allowed by the charter, and the grain shipments from Ironkeep had slowed to a trickle. The Crescent Compact had begun to mint its own coins, stamped with the image of a crescent moon and a single star.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-ANN-167 | Year: SA 432]
The Salt Courts met in secret and decreed that any polity found in violation of the charter would be stripped of its banner. No one knew what this meant, but the threat was enough to keep the peace for a time.
#+end_quote

In Harborhold, the Harbor League had begun to build its own fleet, ostensibly for trade but clearly for war. The Road-Wardens reported seeing new ships under construction, their hulls painted black and their sails dyed red.

** The Rise of the Crescent Compact

The Crescent Compact, under the leadership of the Seal-Keeper Alara Moon, began to assert itself as the true heir to the Fifth Banner. Alara claimed that the Partition Charter had been a mistake, that the empire could only be ruled by one banner, and that the Crescent Compact was the only polity with the right to fly it.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-ANN-173 | Year: SA 434]
The Crescent Compact raised its banner over the Mast-City, and the Salt Courts did not object. The banner was white with a black crescent and a single silver star.
#+end_quote

The other polities watched in silence, each calculating its own next move. Flagistan began to build its own fleet, Ironkeep its own army, and Harborhold its own network of spies.

** The Child-Hostages Come of Age

In SA 437, the child-hostages came of age. Kharad Tines, now a man, was named the heir to the Crescent Compact. Vespera Salt, now a woman, was named the heir to the Iron League. The other two child-hostages, whose names have been lost to history, were named the heirs to Flagistan and Harborhold.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-ANN-181 | Year: SA 437]
The child-hostages were brought before the Salt Courts and sworn to uphold the Partition Charter. They swore on their honor, on their lives, and on the memory of their unknown parents.
#+end_quote

But the oaths were hollow. Each child-hostage had grown up in a different court, with different customs, different beliefs, and different ambitions. They had been raised to be loyal to their host polity, not to the empire.

** The War of the Four Banners

In SA 439, the War of the Four Banners began. It started with a dispute over a salt mine in the Dry Seam, but it quickly escalated into a full-scale war. The Crescent Compact, led by Kharad Tines, attacked Flagistan. The Iron League, led by Vespera Salt, attacked Harborhold. And the Harbor League, whose leader is unknown, attacked Ironkeep.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-ANN-189 | Year: SA 439]
The War of the Four Banners began with a skirmish over a salt mine. By the end of the day, four banners were flying over the same gate.
#+end_quote

The war lasted for three years, and it was fought with a ferocity that shocked even the most hardened veterans. The Road-Wardens reported seeing entire cities burned to the ground, their populations slaughtered or enslaved.

** The End of the Partition

In SA 442, the Partition Charter was officially dissolved. The Salt Courts declared that the empire could no longer be held together by a single banner, and that each polity must now rule itself. The child-hostages, now grown, were given the choice to return to their birth families or to remain in their host courts.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-ANN-195 | Year: SA 442]
The Salt Courts declared the Partition Charter null and void. The child-hostages were given the choice to return to their birth families or to remain in their host courts.
#+end_quote

Kharad Tines chose to remain in the Crescent Compact, where he was crowned the First Crescent King. Vespera Salt chose to return to the Iron League, where she was named the Iron Queen. The other two child-hostages, whose names have been lost to history, made their own choices, and their fates are unknown.

** The Aftermath

The aftermath of the War of the Four Banners was a time of great suffering. The empire had been torn apart, and the polities that remained were weak and divided. The Road-Wardens reported seeing entire regions depopulated, their fields left to rot, their cities left to ruin.

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-SA-B2-ANN-201 | Year: SA 443]
The aftermath of the War of the Four Banners was a time of great suffering. The empire had been torn apart, and the polities that remained were weak and divided.
#+end_quote

But even in the darkest of times, there were those who remembered the old empire, who dreamed of a time when the banners flew together, when the Salt Courts ruled with wisdom and justice. And in the years that followed, these dreamers would begin to weave a new story, a story of hope and renewal, a story that would one day become the legend of the Last Banner.

** The Last Banner

In the years that followed, the legend of the Last Banner began to spread. It was said that the Last Banner was the true heir to the Fifth Banner, that it had been hidden away during the War of the Four Banners, and that it would one day return to unite the empire once more.

#+begin_quote

The legend of the Last Banner began to spread, and with it came hope. The people began to dream of a time when the banners would fly together again, when the Salt Courts would rule with wisdom and justice, and when the empire would be whole once more.
#+end_quote

But the legend of the Last Banner was just that—a legend. The empire was gone, and it would never return. The banners had fallen, and they would never rise again. The Salt Courts had been dissolved, and they would never be reformed. The Road-Wardens had been disbanded, and they would never be reconstituted. The child-hostages had grown up and taken their places as rulers, and they would never be children again.

And so the world moved on, and the legend of the Last Banner became just another story, another tale to be told around the fire, another dream to be dreamed in the dark. But for those who remembered the old empire, who had seen the banners fly and the Salt Courts rule, the legend of the Last Banner would always be more than just a story. It would be a reminder of what had been lost, of what had been taken away, and of what might one day be regained.



* Arc 1: Iron Roads and Surface Doctrine
[Year Span: TA 001 - TA 030]

In the thirtieth year of the Salt Moon's wane, the Mast-City of Flagistan looked inward. The salt roads had brought coin, and the sea had brought trade, but the land—vast, uncharted, and stubborn—demanded a different faith. The Salt Court decreed that roads must be measured, not by leagues, but by the number of axles they could bear, the tons they could haul, the days they could shorten between mast and field. Thus began the Iron Roads, and with them, the Surface Doctrine.

** The Cartographer's Creed
#+begin_quote

The Seal-Keeper's office grew fat with scrolls, each one a testament to the empire's reach. But the land, vast and wild, resisted reduction. * Arc 3: Lantern Riots to Border Fires

** The Flame of Lawlessness

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-LA-B3-ANN-001 | Year: LA 124]
On the first night of the Lantern Feast, five lanterns were found unlit in the eastern quarter; by dawn, every gate to the Salt Docks had been broken and the stores set aflame.
#+end_quote

The lanterns of the Feast had always been more than ritual. They marked the passage from the old year to the new, and their lighting was said to bind the city's debts to the sea. When five failed to burn in the quarter known as the Dry Steps, the omission was not ignored. By the second watch, men had gathered in the market square, whispering that the Seal-Keeper had withheld the sacred oil as punishment for unpaid harbor dues. The rumor spread through the alleys faster than fire, and by the time the lantern-lighters returned with fresh wicks, the crowd had armed itself with oars and net-weights.

The first stones fell at the gate of the Salt Court. The second volley shattered the glass of the ledger house. Within the hour, the ledgers themselves were dragged into the street and burned, their pages curling like black tongues. The banner above the court was torn down, and a new cloth—white with a single red stripe—was hoisted in its place. No one knew who had sewn it, but all understood its meaning: the old law was dead, and a new one would be made from the ashes.

The Banner-Lord's guard arrived at dawn, but the streets were already empty. The rioters had vanished into the city, leaving only the smell of burning paper and the silence of a law that no longer held.

** The Council of Three Waters

#+begin_quote
[Canon: FE-LA-B3-ANN-015 | Year: LA 125]

#+end_quote

In the weeks that followed, the city did not fall into chaos, but into a strange quiet. The Banner-Lord remained in his hall, but his orders were no longer carried out. The Salt Courts sat empty, their clerks dismissed or fled. The only authority that remained was the one that walked the streets in plain clothes, carrying no seal and no sword, only a small red lantern that marked them as neutral.

From this vacuum rose the Council of Three Waters. The name came from the place where they first met: the point where the Black River met the Salt Fork, a place sacred to no one and claimed by all. The three men who formed the council were not nobles, nor were they merchants. They were a lantern-lighter, a dockworker, and a former guard who had thrown down his sword rather than fire on the rioters.

Their first act was to declare a moratorium on all debts. Their second was to order the reopening of the docks, but without tariffs. Their third was to send a message to the Banner-Lord: the city would govern itself until a new banner could be agreed upon by all.

The Banner-Lord did not reply. Instead, he sent his guard to arrest the council. They were taken at dawn, as they met at the river confluence. But when the guard arrived, they found not three men, but three hundred. The people had come to protect their council, and the guard, seeing the size of the crowd, lowered their weapons and withdrew.

** The Last Year

#+begin_quote

#+end_quote

The last year began with a fire on the eastern plain that did not go out. It burned for days, and when it finally died, it left behind a blackened scar that stretched to the horizon. The people of the city watched it from the walls, but they did not fear it. They had seen too many fires by then, and they had learned that fire did not always bring destruction. Sometimes, it brought renewal.

But this fire was different. It did not stop at the plain. It moved through the hills, through the forests, through the villages that lay between the city and the mountains. It burned the granaries and the barns, the mills and the bridges. It left the people who lived there with nothing but the clothes on their backs and the ashes of their homes.

The Council of Three Waters met for the last time at the river confluence. They did not bring a banner, nor a ledger, nor a blade. They brought only their lanterns, and they lit them in the darkness. They spoke of the fires, of the people who had lost everything, of the Banner-Lord who had done nothing. They spoke of the city that had tried to govern itself, and of the world that had refused to let it.

When they were done, they put out their lanterns and went their separate ways. The city did not need a council anymore. It needed only to survive.

The fires reached the city walls in the autumn of the last year. They came not as an army, but as a tide, flowing over the fields and through the forests, leaving nothing but ash in their wake. The people of the city watched from the walls, but no one fired a shot, no one raised a banner. They simply waited for the end.

And when the end came, it was not with a fire, but with a silence. The fires had burned themselves out, and the world beyond the walls was nothing but a wasteland of ash and smoke. The city stood alone, its banner still flying, its people still alive, but its future gone.

The last year ended with the city still standing, but the world around it was gone. The fires had taken everything, and the people of the city knew that they could not rebuild what had been lost. They could only wait for the next fire, and the next, until there was nothing left to burn.
